What waits inside the cover

Most books about Agile and Scrum read like software manuals. If you run an operations desk, a logistics team, a service department, or a mid-sized business, they rarely sound like your Monday morning.

Part One: The Story

At Ashby & Co. in Hamburg, the spare parts desk is quietly sinking. Overdue orders keep climbing, tempers are fraying, and yet every weekly review around the mahogany table stays politely green. George has little patience for management slogans, and even less for pretending everything is fine. Watch what happens when he and his team finally stop hiding the mess, pin the real number on the wall, and test Scrum against real daily pressure.

Part Two: The Field Guide

When the story finishes, the book does not leave you guessing. A concise, plain-English Field Guide at the back translates every habit from the novel into concrete steps for non-IT teams: how to set up a visible board, how to run short focused cycles, and how to stop status meetings from turning back into polite theatre.

A Note from the Author

This book began as an experiment on myself. Over the years, I have noticed two things. First, I get restless without a new challenge. Second, dry frameworks slide right off my brain unless they are wrapped in a story about real people making a mess of things. At the same time, friends and family kept telling me over dinner that Scrum is only for software people in hoodies. That is simply wrong, and writing about a spare parts desk in Hamburg felt like a good way to prove it.

In my own career, I have watched teams cling to polite fiction for months, dutifully colouring status reports green while everyone in the room knew the ship was already taking on water. Nothing kills good people faster than pretending a bad plan works. Yet I have also seen the exact moment someone finally sighs, puts the real, ugly number on the wall, and admits they are stuck. Nobody dies of shame. Instead, the shoulders drop, the excuses stop, and the actual work begins.

Of everyone at that mahogany table, George is closest to me, though perhaps with a hint of Paul on a good day. Like George, I distrust buzzwords on sight and learn best by rolling up my sleeves and testing things against reality. Just so nobody in Hamburg or anywhere else starts checking old personnel files, Ashby & Co., the Corvane Cruise Group, Varnholt Process Services, and every person in these pages are entirely invented.

When you close this book, skip the grand announcements. On Monday morning, buy a pack of index cards, pick the one problem your team has been stepping around all month, and put it on the wall where nobody can ignore it. Then take three steps back and listen.

Carlos Lucas

Scrum Beyond IT: Frequently Asked Questions

Straight answers about the book, who it is written for, and how Scrum fits into everyday work outside software.

Can Scrum actually work outside IT?

Yes. Scrum has very little to do with writing code. At its core, it is a simple discipline for facing messy, changing work in short cycles: make the real backlog visible, focus on what matters most this week, review honest results, and adjust before months go by. Teams in operations, spare parts, customer service, finance, and administration use these habits every day.

Is The Number on the Wall a novel or a textbook?

It is both. The main part of the book is a story set at a spare parts desk in Hamburg, following George and his colleagues as they stop colouring status reports green and figure out a better way to work. At the back, a concise Field Guide turns the lessons from the story into plain, step-by-step notes you can try with your own team on Monday morning.

Who should read this book?

Team leads, department heads, operations managers, and anyone outside IT who is tired of polite meetings that hide real problems. Readers who enjoyed The Phoenix Project, The Goal, or The Five Dysfunctions of a Team will feel right at home. Scrum Masters and Agile Coaches also use it as a practical gift for sceptical business colleagues.

What is "the Number on the Wall"?

It is the one uncomfortable fact a team finally stops hiding behind green status slides, represented on the cover by a red 38 pinned to a plain index card. Putting that number in plain sight ends the polite fiction, lowers the tension in the room, and lets everyone focus on fixing the actual problem.

About the Author
