From the Book

A Note from the Author

This book began as an experiment on myself. Over the years, I have noticed two things. First, I get restless without a new challenge. Second, dry frameworks slide right off my brain unless they are wrapped in a story about real people making a mess of things. At the same time, friends and family kept telling me over dinner that Scrum is only for software people in hoodies. That is simply wrong, and writing about a spare parts desk in Hamburg felt like a good way to prove it.

In my own career, I have watched teams cling to polite fiction for months, dutifully colouring status reports green while everyone in the room knew the ship was already taking on water. Nothing kills good people faster than pretending a bad plan works. Yet I have also seen the exact moment someone finally sighs, puts the real, ugly number on the wall, and admits they are stuck. Nobody dies of shame. Instead, the shoulders drop, the excuses stop, and the actual work begins.

Of everyone at that mahogany table, George is closest to me, though perhaps with a hint of Paul on a good day. Like George, I distrust buzzwords on sight and learn best by rolling up my sleeves and testing things against reality. Just so nobody in Hamburg or anywhere else starts checking old personnel files, Ashby & Co., the Corvane Cruise Group, Varnholt Process Services, and every person in these pages are entirely invented.

When you close this book, skip the grand announcements. On Monday morning, buy a pack of index cards, pick the one problem your team has been stepping around all month, and put it on the wall where nobody can ignore it. Then take three steps back and listen.

In Practice

Scrum Beyond IT – Frequently Asked Questions

Direct answers on what the book covers, who it is written for, and how Scrum works outside software development.

01 Can Scrum actually work outside IT?

Yes. Scrum is not a software-engineering method—it is a lightweight empirical framework for handling complex, fast-changing work in short cycles. Teams in operations, spare-parts logistics, customer service, administration, finance, and marketing use its core habits—making real bottlenecks visible, working in short timeboxes, inspecting actual results, and adapting—without writing a single line of code.

02 Is The Number on the Wall a novel or a textbook?

It is both. The main part is a fast-paced business novel set at a spare-parts desk inside Ashby & Co. in Hamburg, following realistic characters as they move from polite status-report fiction to honest teamwork. At the back of the book, a concise Field Guide distils every Scrum role, event, and habit from the story into plain-English instructions you can use on Monday morning.

03 Who should read this book?

Team leads, department heads, operations managers, project coordinators, and everyday practitioners outside software development who want practical results rather than abstract Agile buzzwords. It is also written for readers who enjoy business novels like The Phoenix Project or The Goal, as well as Scrum Masters looking for an accessible way to introduce Scrum to business teams.

04 What is "the Number on the Wall"?

It is the single, uncomfortable reality a team stops hiding behind green status reports—symbolised on the cover by a handwritten "38" pinned to an index card. Putting that real number on the wall replaces blame and polite excuses with shared visibility, allowing a team to stop pretending a broken plan works and start fixing what matters most.

Yes to both. The English First Edition launches first, with German, Spanish, and Portuguese editions planned next. A sequel—More Trouble Ahead—is already in development, continuing the story as the team faces new organisational challenges beyond its first breakthrough. About the Author

Carlos Lucas is a natural border-crosser who has spent more than two decades moving between business and technology, helping teams solve stubborn problems by looking outside the obvious box. Always drawn to new challenges, he believes the only real way to understand a method is to test it in practice, make mistakes, and learn from experience. He lives in the north of Germany near Hamburg. While George bakes bread on Saturdays to clear his head, Carlos prefers chasing new experiences or taking a brisk walk around the Alster lake or along the Elbe. You can find him at linkedin.com/in/it-for-humans/ or join his reader list at carloslucas.de/newsletter.
