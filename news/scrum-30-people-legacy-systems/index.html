Scrum with 30 People, 12 Legacy Systems, and Daily Fires

Textbook Scrum assumes one small, cross-functional team working on one modern product without constant interruptions. Real operations rarely look like that. Here is a practical blueprint for introducing Scrum in a 30-person hybrid unit where business and technical specialists juggle long-term projects, 12 complex legacy systems, tight schedules, planned holidays, and unpredictable daily fires.

By Carlos Lucas · Reading time: 12 minutes · Hypothetical Field Scenario

1. Why Textbook Scrum Breaks on Day One

Picture a realistic, everyday scenario that exists in hundreds of mid-sized and large organisations.

An operational division has around 30 people, roughly split into two halves. One half is the business side: process specialists, domain coordinators, and operational leads who know the rules, the customers, and the regulatory deadlines. The other half is technically oriented: application managers, interface specialists, and system engineers who keep a sprawling landscape of 12 complex legacy systems running.

Three realities define their week:

  1. Extreme heterogeneity: Skills, seniority, and daily tasks vary wildly across the 30 people.
  2. Bus-Factor 1 bottlenecks: For several critical legacy systems, there are only one or two people in the entire company who truly understand how the system behaves under stress. Whenever one of them takes two weeks of annual leave or calls in sick, progress stalls.
  3. Two competing worlds of work: Long-term strategic projects sit side by side with urgent daily incidents, ad-hoc business questions, and unexpected operational fires, while everyone already feels they have zero spare time.

If you walk into this environment and announce textbook Scrum, it will collapse within three weeks. A 30-person Daily Scrum turns into an agonising 45-minute status report where 27 people zone out. Splitting everyone into rigid cross-functional teams fails because you cannot clone the one specialist who knows Legacy System C. Locking the team into a two-week Sprint commitment fails the moment a high-priority production issue hits on Tuesday morning.

Does that mean Scrum cannot work here? No. It means you have to stop treating Scrum as a rigid software script and start using its underlying mechanics: transparency, empiricism, small batches, and clear ownership.

2. Separate Planned Work from Daily Fires (Dual-Track Scrumban)

The single biggest reason agile rollouts die in hybrid departments is invisible, unplanned work. When an urgent operational problem lands directly on a specialist's desk via email, chat, or a quick hallway conversation, it silently destroys the Sprint.

The fix is a Two-Speed Operating Model (Dual-Track Scrumban). Every piece of work, whether it is a six-month regulatory change or a sudden system hiccup, enters through one central intake funnel. Nothing bypasses the board.

Every morning, a 10-minute Triage (led by the Product Owner and one technical lead) sorts new incoming items into two distinct tracks:

  • Track A (Planned Value / 2-Week Sprint): Multi-day improvements, project milestones, and planned system changes. This track gets a protected share of team capacity (typically 60% to 70% to start, including a deliberate slice for pairing and handover) and a clear business Sprint Goal.
  • Track B (Fast-Lane / Kanban Flow): Urgent operational incidents, ad-hoc business requests, and small fixes. This track gets a dedicated capacity buffer (30% to 40%), prioritised strictly by urgency and governed by hard Work-in-Progress (WIP) limits.
Practitioner Rule: Never hide the buffer.
If 35% of your real week goes into keeping the lights on, plan your Sprint for 65% capacity. Teams lose trust in Scrum when management pretends the 35% does not exist and asks for 100% project delivery on top.

The 3-Question Fast-Lane Filter (Stopping Fake Urgency)

Without clear entry rules, every stakeholder will label their request "urgent" and flood Track B. During the 10-minute Triage, an item enters the Fast-Lane only if at least one answer is yes:

  1. Active impact today: Is a customer, live service, or billing/operational process broken right now?
  2. Hard external deadline: Is there a binding legal, regulatory, or contractual deadline within the next five working days?
  3. Team blocker: Is this issue stopping two or more colleagues from continuing their work today?

If all three answers are no, the request is not an emergency. Place it calmly into Track A's prioritised backlog for the next Sprint.

3. Structure 30 People Without Breaking Your Bottlenecks

You cannot run one 30-person Scrum team. At the same time, you cannot pretend every team has its own expert for all 12 legacy systems.

Instead of splitting along the old wall ("Business Department" vs. "Technical Department"), reorganise the work around value streams or business domains while treating rare system specialists honestly:

  1. One Unified Product Owner (PO): Business and technology share one single prioritised portfolio backlog. Two separate backlogs guarantee conflicting priorities and endless escalation meetings.
  2. Three Mixed Domain Teams (8 to 9 people each): Each team blends business specialists and technical generalists around a coherent operational area (for example: Customer Operations, Core Processing, Reporting and Compliance).
  3. One Shared Legacy Specialist Pool (3 to 5 people): The rare experts who own Bus-Factor 1 systems are not locked inside a single domain team. They operate as a shared enabler pool with two clear rules:
    • They hold reserved support slots that domain teams book during weekly Refinement (not by ambush on Thursday afternoon).
    • Every complex ticket on a 1-expert system triggers mandatory pair-working with a colleague from the requesting team, steadily building an 80/20 Safe-Hands Deputy for every core system. Knowledge transfer is no longer a vague wish; it is built into the workflow.

4. Three Daily Operating Rules That Keep the Peace

Structure on paper means nothing if daily habits stay chaotic. Three simple guardrails protect focus without creating bureaucracy:

1. The Rotating "First Responder" Role

In every Sprint, each domain team (and the specialist pool) designates one rotating person as the First Responder (often nicknamed the "Firefighter" or "Batman"). Every unplanned ad-hoc request goes to this person first. The rest of the team stays shielded and works on the Sprint Goal. If the week is calm, the First Responder picks up small maintenance or documentation tasks. If a major fire breaks out, a second colleague steps in deliberately rather than everyone panicking at once.

2. Hard WIP Limits (Max 2 Items Per Person)

On the Kanban Fast-Lane, no individual may have more than two active cards in progress at the same time. When a legacy expert already has two cards in motion, nobody is allowed to drop a third urgent task on them. Either one active card gets finished, or a stakeholder explicitly decides which active card gets paused. Suddenly, trade-offs become visible instead of invisible stress.

3. A Lean Weekly Cadence (Cut Old Meetings First)

Hybrid teams already suffer from meeting fatigue. Before introducing Scrum events, cancel redundant weekly status meetings and replace them with three tight touchpoints:

Cadence Event Who Attends Concrete Focus
Daily (15 min) Board Sync Each Domain Team Not a status report. Three questions only: Which card is stuck? Do we need a shared specialist today? Is an ad-hoc fire threatening the Sprint Goal?
Weekly (45 min) Joint Refinement PO, Business & Tech Reps, Shared Specialists The most important meeting. Slice long-term work into small pieces, spot legacy dependencies, and check upcoming holidays before the Sprint starts.
Every 2 Weeks (60 min) Review & Retro All Teams + Key Stakeholders 30 min Review: Show what is genuinely finished (no slide decks). 30 min Retro: Measure how much time went into fires and adjust the capacity split (for example, 70/30 to 60/40).

5. Surviving Zero Spare Time, Annual Leave, and Sick Days

Pair-working and shared specialist pools work brilliantly over six to twelve months. Yet operational leaders face two sharper, immediate objections on Monday morning: "We have zero spare time to document or pair right now" and "What happens when our key Legacy System C specialist takes two weeks of holiday next month or calls in sick on Tuesday?"

If your process collapses every time one expert steps away, teams quickly abandon Scrum and revert to panic heroics. Four practical habits keep the entire unit moving without burning anyone out.

1. Fund Resilience Explicitly (The 60/30/10 Rule and the 5-Minute Documentation Rule)

Knowledge transfer dies whenever leaders expect people to do it "on the side" on top of 100% workload. Once your Dual-Track board is running, carve an explicit 10% Resilience & Pairing slice out of Track A so your real capacity plan reads roughly 60% Planned Sprint / 10% Pairing & Handover / 30% Fast-Lane. Put pairing and handover cards directly onto the board as first-class Sprint work.

To avoid wasting scarce hours on sprawling manuals nobody maintains, enforce the 5-Minute Documentation Rule: ban 15-page wikis. Whenever a specialist fixes a recurring legacy glitch or runs a tricky monthly job, they hit record on a five-minute screen-share while doing the live work and write five bullet points underneath. Documentation happens inside real work at nearly zero extra time cost.

2. Build 80/20 "Safe-Hands Deputies" Instead of Trying to Clone Experts

You do not need two 15-year veterans for every one of your 12 legacy systems. That takes years and blows every budget. What you actually need is one Primary Owner and one Safe-Hands Deputy per critical system. The Deputy cannot redesign the database schema, and does not need to. The Deputy only needs the 20% of operational know-how that covers 80% of daily incidents: how to check logs, restart stuck batch jobs, apply standard workarounds, and judge whether an issue can safely wait until Monday.

Pin a simple System Coverage Grid next to your board listing all 12 systems with three columns: Primary Owner, Safe-Hands Deputy, and Red Flag (Bus-Factor 1). Every empty Deputy slot is a real Number on the Wall that guides where your 10% pairing budget goes next.

3. Keep a 1-Page Legacy System Emergency Card

Give every Safe-Hands Deputy one plain, single-page reference per legacy system (30 minutes to draft once, updated whenever something surprises the team):

Emergency Card Field What Goes on the Page (Plain Language Only)
1. Top 5 Recurring Failures The five issues that actually happen every quarter, how to recognise them, and the exact safe step-by-step fix or restart sequence.
2. Logs, Jobs & Access Exact server paths, scheduled job names, dashboard URLs, and which shared role or permission group the Deputy needs verified before leave starts.
3. Manual Business Workaround If the system stays down for 24 hours, how do operations keep serving customers manually (spreadsheet bridge, temporary queue, fallback dispatch)?
4. Escalation & Vendor Contact External vendor support hotline, contract or SLA reference number, and the internal senior peer who can assist on a true outage.
5. Explicitly Safe to Wait A short list of non-critical requests (minor field changes, ad-hoc reports, cosmetic fixes) that the Product Owner has pre-approved to park until the specialist returns.

4. Run Leave-Proof Sprint Planning and the 45-Minute Handover

Put planned annual leave onto the shared board four to six weeks ahead (a two-Sprint look-ahead). During weekly Refinement, apply one blunt Definition of Ready Dependency Check before pulling any Track A card into a Sprint: "If Specialist X is off sick tomorrow, does this card freeze the rest of the team?" If yes, split out the specialist's piece for the prior Sprint, pair on it early, or pick work touching the other 11 systems instead.

The 4-Step Pre-Leave Handover Checklist (45 Minutes Total)

  1. Two weeks before leave: Pull zero risky changes or major releases touching that specialist's system into the overlapping Sprint. No big deployments 48 hours before departure.
  2. One week before leave: Specialist and Safe-Hands Deputy pair on one live routine ticket, confirm login permissions actually work, and refresh the 1-Page Emergency Card.
  3. During leave (3-Tier Triage): Tier 1 (non-urgent tweaks and feature requests) are parked visibly on the board until return; Tier 2 (routine incidents) are handled by the Deputy using the Emergency Card; Tier 3 (true production outage) triggers the pre-arranged vendor escalation or manual business workaround, never calling the colleague on holiday as Plan A.
  4. First Retro after return (5-Minute Stress Test): Spend five minutes asking: "What stalled or nearly broke while X was away?" Turn every gap into one 30-minute shadowing card for the next Sprint.
Practitioner Rule: Treat absence as a free stress test.
A planned two-week holiday or an unexpected sick day is not a failure of Scrum. It is the cheapest real-world audit your organisation will ever get. Fix the two gaps it exposes each time, and within three leave cycles your most fragile bottlenecks lose their sting.

6. The 4-Week Soft Launch Without a Big Bang

Never start by rolling out new titles, agile posters, and a mandatory 30-person kickoff workshop. In sceptical, heterogeneous teams, buzzwords trigger immediate antibodies. Start with visibility and earn trust step by step.

  • Week 1: Put All Work on One Wall. Build one shared board (physical or digital) with two horizontal swimlanes: Urgent / Daily Operations on top, Planned Projects below. Introduce one simple rule: if it is not on the board, it does not exist.
  • Week 2: Activate the First Responder and WIP Limits. Assign the first rotating First Responder, apply the 3-Question Fast-Lane Filter, and enforce the 2-item WIP limit. Let the team experience what it feels like to finish work instead of starting ten things at once.
  • Week 3: Run the First 2-Week Sprint. Pull only realistic planned items into the lower lane, respecting your honest ad-hoc buffer and checking upcoming holidays two Sprints ahead. Deliver 60% reliably rather than overpromising 100% and failing.
  • Week 4: Measure Real Numbers, Start Pairing, and Name Your First Deputy. In your first Retrospective, look at the actual ratio of fires to planned work. Mark every Bus-Factor 1 legacy system in red, schedule your very first pair-working ticket, and draft one 1-Page Emergency Card with a Safe-Hands Deputy.

7. Field Guide for Resistance, Setbacks, and Friction

Every real rollout hits turbulence between Week 3 and Week 8. The difference between an experienced practitioner and a textbook theorist is how you handle friction when people push back.

Resistance 1: The Indispensable Specialist

"I do not have time to explain this to someone else. It takes me 20 minutes alone and two hours if I pair."

  • Why it happens: Usually a mix of genuine workload pressure and quiet pride in being the only person who can save the day.
  • Practitioner counter-move: Do not lecture them about "team spirit." Acknowledge that pairing is slower today. Then reframe it around their own pain: "Right now, you cannot take two weeks of uninterrupted holiday without your phone ringing. Pairing is not extra work; it is how we buy back your peace of mind." Start with a five-minute recorded screen-share or a single 30-minute shadowing session on a routine ticket, booked inside the 10% resilience slice, not a full-week overhaul.

Resistance 2: Stakeholders Bypassing the Board

"Can you just do me a quick 5-minute favour? No need to put a ticket in the system."

  • Why it happens: Direct personal favours have worked for years, and the new board feels slower to the requester.
  • Practitioner counter-move: Train the team to use one calm, polite standard sentence: "I am happy to help. Let us put a quick card on the board and check with the Product Owner which current task we should pause for it." Never say "no" aggressively; simply make the trade-off visible. Once stakeholders see that their favour pushes back another priority they also care about, back-channel requests drop sharply.

Setback 1: A Major Production Outage Destroys the Sprint

In Week 2, a core legacy system fails for two days. Half the Sprint Goal is missed. Sceptics immediately say, "See? Scrum does not work in our department."

  • Practitioner counter-move: Stay completely calm and celebrate the transparency. Point at the board and say: "Scrum did not cause the outage. For the first time, the board shows management in black and white what that legacy failure actually cost our project timeline." Adjust the remaining Sprint scope openly with the PO, log the root cause as a future backlog item, and move on without drama.

Setback 2: Business and Tech Talk Past Each Other in Refinement

Business colleagues describe vague goals; technical colleagues drown the room in database fields and interface logs. Both sides leave frustrated.

  • Practitioner counter-move: Ban solution talk for the first 10 minutes of any Refinement item. Ask the business side one plain question: "When this is finished next Friday, what concrete thing can a customer or colleague do that they cannot do today?" Write that single sentence at the top of the card. Only then let the technical specialists list which legacy systems are touched and where the hidden risks sit.

Setback 3: "This Is Just Extra Meeting Bureaucracy"

Experienced operational staff resent sitting in recurring meetings when their inbox is overflowing.

  • Practitioner counter-move: Enforce a strict One-In, One-Out rule for meetings. Before you add a 15-minute Daily Sync, kill the old 60-minute weekly Monday status meeting. Timebox every event ruthlessly. End a 15-minute Daily at minute 14 even if someone is mid-story, and move detailed troubleshooting into an optional two-person huddle right after. When people see you respect their calendar, scepticism fades fast.

Setback 4: A Core Specialist Calls in Sick Mid-Sprint

On Wednesday morning, the only deep expert for Legacy System F calls in sick for the rest of the week. Two Sprint cards and one live interface depend on them.

  • Practitioner counter-move: Do not freeze the board or call the sick colleague at home. Immediately apply your 3-Tier Triage: move the non-urgent Sprint cards into a visible Parked (Waiting on System F) lane so the PO can pull clean substitute work from the backlog; hand any live operational hiccup to the Safe-Hands Deputy with the 1-Page Emergency Card; and keep the remaining 29 people delivering against the rest of the Sprint Goal. In the next Retro, spend five minutes closing whichever know-how gap hurt most.
The Practitioner's Compass:
Do not try to convince all 30 people at once. Find the 4 or 5 respected pragmatists across business and tech who are tired of daily chaos. Solve one real pain point with them on a visible board. Nothing converts sceptics faster than watching a neighbouring team leave work on time on a Friday.

Quick Practitioner Answers

Can you run Scrum with 30 people in one team?

No. A single 30-person Daily Scrum turns into a 45-minute status report where most colleagues disengage. Instead, organise the unit around one Unified Product Owner, three mixed domain teams of 8 to 9 people blending business and technical roles, and a small shared legacy specialist pool of 3 to 5 rare experts.

How do you handle a single legacy system expert in Scrum?

Keep rare Bus-Factor 1 experts in a shared enabler pool rather than locking them inside one domain team. Protect their focus with reserved support slots booked during weekly Refinement, enforce mandatory pair-working on complex tickets, and assign a Safe-Hands Deputy backed by a one-page Emergency Card.

How do you protect a Sprint from daily unplanned ad-hoc work?

Use Dual-Track Scrumban. Route all incoming work through a daily 10-minute triage using strict Fast-Lane entry criteria, reserve an honest capacity buffer (typically 30% to 40%) for urgent operational flow, assign a rotating First Responder each Sprint, and cap active Fast-Lane work at two cards per person.

How do you keep Scrum running when a key legacy specialist goes on annual leave or calls in sick?

Plan around absences two Sprints ahead, avoid risky changes on that system right before leave, and pair the specialist with an 80/20 Safe-Hands Deputy who holds a one-page Emergency Card. During the absence, use a three-tier triage rule: park non-urgent changes, let the Deputy handle routine incidents, and use a pre-agreed vendor escalation or manual workaround for true outages.

Monday Morning Checklist

Seven Steps to Test This Without Asking for Permission

  1. Pick one painful cross-department workflow where business and tech collide every week.
  2. Cancel one redundant weekly status meeting first so people feel time returning to their calendar.
  3. Put both the planned work and the daily fires onto one single visible board, guarded by the 3-Question Fast-Lane Filter.
  4. Name one rotating First Responder for the week so everyone else gets uninterrupted focus blocks.
  5. Cap active Fast-Lane work at two cards per person and make every priority trade-off visible to stakeholders.
  6. Mark every Bus-Factor 1 legacy system in red, name one Safe-Hands Deputy, and draft a 1-Page Emergency Card before the next holiday starts.
  7. After two weeks, count the honest split between fires, planned projects, and pairing, and pin that real number on the wall.
