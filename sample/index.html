Chapter 1: Thirty-Eight

Opening chapter from The Number on the Wall: A Business Novel About Scrum Beyond IT (Paperback ISBN 9798146680767), set at the Hamburg spare-parts desk of Ashby & Co.

At ten past three in the morning, somewhere in the Bay of Biscay, the cold store of the main galley stopped humming.

Tomasz heard it in his sleep. Thirty-one years at sea teach a man to hear the absence of a sound, and he was standing in the engine control room in his socks before he was properly awake. The compressor had failed. He had known since Thursday that it would. On Thursday he had ordered the replacement from the oldest and most respectable spare parts house in Europe, the one whose founder's portrait he had once seen in a lobby, and he had not had an answer.

He wrote to them again, at a quarter past three, in capital letters this time. Then he went down to the galley with a torch and stood among three thousand people's worth of food, and did the sum in his head. The small backup unit would keep one cold room alive, just. Not the main store. Not for the crossing that began on Friday evening, with three thousand passengers and nothing fresh on board unless that part arrived first.

If.

At headquarters in Hamburg, far to the north, his message arrived in an inbox that nobody had opened since Friday, on top of his first one. In the same system, a request from another ship crossed the forty-eight-hour line, and a small program added one to a number on a screen on the fourth floor.

The number became 31.

Nobody saw it happen. The only person who would have understood what it meant was lying awake in a rented flat across the city, staring at the ceiling, with his hands pressed flat against his chest to stop them shaking.

* * *

The fourth-floor conference room smelled of rain and old paper. Outside, the November wind threw itself against the glass. Inside, nobody had moved for a full minute.

On the wall behind the head of the table, a screen showed a single number in red.

Thirty-eight.

Everybody in the room knew what it counted. Ashby & Co. had supplied spare parts to ships since 1889, longer than almost anyone else in the trade. Its founder, a Scottish ship's engineer who had arrived in Hamburg with a toolbox and a debt, still looked down from his portrait in the lobby. To this day the house did its business in his language, and its desks were filled with people from a dozen countries. Its customers were divided into sectors, and Sector 4 was the cruise fleet of the Corvane Cruise Group, the biggest customer the house had ever had. Thirty-eight urgent requests from those ships had waited more than forty-eight hours without an answer. Somewhere at sea or in port, thirty-eight chief engineers did not know whether their part was coming. A ship does not wait. It sails on schedule, with thousands of passengers on board, with the part or without it. Every request that crossed the forty-eight-hour line went automatically to the executive floor. At eight o'clock that morning, the number had been 31.

Janet sat at the head of the long mahogany table and tapped her pen against her notebook, slowly, to keep her hand from shaking. She pushed her glasses up her nose and looked at the five people she was supposed to lead. They had chosen their seats as far from each other as the table allowed.

She was thirty-six. She had taken over the customer service division in the middle of August, with a box of leadership books, a dog called Kailey, and the quiet conviction that a team could be a place people wanted to come back to on a Monday. Eleven weeks later, she was no longer sure she had ever seen one.

George sat on her right, as he had sat on the right of every manager for nearly thirty years. He was broad and heavy-shouldered, with thinning hair and a frown that seemed to have been there since birth. He baked his own bread every Saturday, the same loaf by the same recipe, and he regarded every new initiative from the fourth floor the way a farmer regards weather: something that would pass, provided one kept one's head down.

Halfway down the table sat Paul, in a sharp jacket over jeans, scrolling through something on his phone as if he had only dropped in on his way to a better meeting. He had a motivational quote for every occasion, and he had never once doubted that he was the cleverest person in the room. Opposite him sat Claire, arms folded so tightly that her knuckles showed. She had already found two wrong figures in the agenda, marked them in red, and laid the sheet face down, squared to the edge of the table. In six years they had not said a kind word to each other. Everyone in the department had paid for it.

Amanda sat next to Claire, which was brave of her. She was twenty-four, the youngest of them, and her corner of the office was a riot of coloured sticky notes and photographs of baby animals. Today she had not smiled once.

And by the door, half in shadow, sat Timothy, the department's data analyst, with his headphones round his neck and his laptop shut in front of him. He had built the report behind the red number on the wall himself. He was paler than usual. His hands were under the table, and they were shaking.

Janet closed her folder with a sharp snap.

'Six weeks,' she said, her voice unusually firm. 'Six weeks after this one. That's all the time we have. If this division doesn't restructure completely and raise customer satisfaction by forty per cent by the end of the quarter, the executive board will dissolve the department and outsource everything.'

The wind threw a handful of rain at the window.

George dropped his pen. 'Forty per cent in six weeks? That's impossible, Janet. Look at that number. We're drowning already. You can't throw away decades of established processes overnight.'

'It gets worse,' Janet said. 'Dr Marcus Vance has been appointed special commissioner for the reorganisation.'

Something went through the room that was not quite a shudder. Everyone in the building knew the name. Dr Vance was the man the board sent when it had already made up its mind and wanted somebody else to sign.

'Vance wants a detailed, two-hundred-page master plan on his desk by Friday,' Janet continued. 'Every single hour of the next six weeks, planned in advance and approved by him. And if a single step fails, which it will, because nobody can plan six weeks to the hour, he'll make sure the blame lands on us.'

'That's insane.' Paul ran a hand through his hair. 'He's setting us up to fail so he can bring in his own consultants.'

'As if you had a better idea,' Claire snapped. 'You talk big about modern management, Paul, but when reality hits, you're useless.'

'At least I look for solutions instead of hiding behind dusty binders.'

In the corner, Timothy had gone the colour of the wall. Janet saw him swallow twice, as if he were about to say something, and then look down at the table.

She looked from one face to the next. If we go on like this for six weeks, she thought, he won't even need to try.

'We're not going to write a two-hundred-page fantasy novel for Dr Vance,' she said quietly. 'We're going to do something different. The company retreat starts tomorrow. Every department is going, and so are we. But we won't be there for trust games. We'll use those four days to learn a way of working that might actually save us. It's called Scrum.'

George stared at her. Then he pointed at the wall.

'A retreat. There are ships lying in port waiting for parts we haven't even confirmed, and you want to take us on a trip.'

'A temporary crew takes over the phones tomorrow morning. They'll hold the line.'

'They won't fix it.'

'No,' Janet said. 'And neither have we. I've watched that number for eleven weeks, George. We've worked evenings. We've worked weekends. It hasn't gone down once.'

George opened his mouth and found nothing to put in it. He rubbed his temples instead. 'Another management buzzword.'

'No. It's the only way out of this that I can see.'

They left one by one, without looking at each other. Janet stayed in her chair until the corridor was quiet.

Behind her, the screen flickered. 39.

Her phone buzzed on the mahogany. One line, from Dr Vance.

Friday. The plan, and the two names.

She turned the phone face down. That was the part she had not told them.
